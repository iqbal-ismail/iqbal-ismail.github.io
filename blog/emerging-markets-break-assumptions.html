Emerging Markets Break Your Assumptions Faster Than Developed Ones

Iqbal K I · July 2026 · 7 min read

Most of the deep reinforcement learning portfolio literature — mine included, until recently — starts with developed-market data. That's a reasonable place to start: liquid, long histories, relatively clean. But it also means most published claims about what these agents "learn" are really claims about what they learn under conditions that are, in a specific sense, unusually forgiving.

Comparing agent behaviour across market-cap segments and emerging-market conditions — work I'm currently finishing — has been a useful corrective to some assumptions I didn't know I was making.

Liquidity assumptions hide in the reward function

A reward function built around frictionless rebalancing implicitly assumes you can execute your desired weights at something close to the observed price. In smaller emerging-market names, that assumption breaks down in ways developed-market backtests rarely expose. An agent trained without any friction penalty will happily propose rebalancing patterns that would move the market against itself if actually executed.

Regime persistence is shorter

One thing that surprised me: volatility regimes in the emerging-market segments of my data tend to be shorter and more abrupt than their developed-market counterparts. An agent that's learned to condition its behaviour on regime persistence — de-risking gradually as volatility builds — has less runway to do that gradually. The policy that works well in one setting doesn't just perform worse in the other; it can fail in a qualitatively different way, over-trading in response to noise it would otherwise have learned to ignore.

What "robust" should actually mean

This is partly why I'm skeptical of robustness claims that rest on a single market or a single time period, no matter how long the backtest window. A policy that's robust across fifteen years of one market has been tested on one draw from a much larger distribution of possible market conditions. Testing across market-cap segments and geographies isn't a nice-to-have extension — it's closer to the actual test of whether an agent has learned something general or just something local.

A model that only works where you trained it hasn't learned a strategy. It's memorized a market.

I go into the empirical detail — and the friction-penalty design that ended up mattering more than I expected — in the manuscripts currently under review. For now, the short version: if your DRL portfolio agent has only ever seen one kind of market, treat any claim about its robustness as provisional.
