Why Deep Reinforcement Learning Might Change Portfolio Management

Iqbal K I · August 2026 · 6 min read

Ask a traditional portfolio model how much to allocate to equities versus bonds, and it will give you an answer built on assumptions: that returns follow a particular distribution, that risk is well approximated by variance, that the relationships between assets today look like the relationships between assets yesterday. Markowitz's mean-variance framework and its descendants have been enormously useful precisely because they make these assumptions explicit. But markets don't always cooperate with them.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) takes a different starting point. Instead of specifying a model of returns and solving for an optimal allocation, you give an agent a reward signal — portfolio return, adjusted for risk — and let it learn a policy through repeated interaction with market data. No distributional assumptions, no fixed rebalancing rule. Just a neural network updating its behaviour based on what worked and what didn't.

What the agent actually learns

In the work I've published on this — most recently on volatility regime-dependent portfolio optimization — the interesting result isn't that a DRL agent can allocate a portfolio. It's how its behaviour shifts once volatility regimes are made explicit in the state it observes. A well-trained agent doesn't apply one static strategy; it learns something closer to a family of strategies, switched in and out as market conditions change.

That's not unlike what a good discretionary portfolio manager does intuitively — de-risking into turbulence, leaning in during calm, stable periods. The difference is that the agent's version of that intuition is learned directly from historical price action, and can be tested, measured, and interrogated in a way that human intuition can't be.

Where it still falls short

None of this means DRL agents are ready to replace human portfolio managers, and I don't think that's the right framing anyway. A few honest limitations, from the empirical work in Indian capital markets and elsewhere:

  • Regime shifts the agent hasn't seen before. A policy trained on historical data can struggle when markets enter genuinely novel conditions — the thing backtests are structurally bad at capturing.
  • Interpretability. It's one thing to observe that an agent de-risks before a drawdown; it's another to explain why in terms a risk committee would accept.
  • Transaction costs and capacity. Agents optimized in simulation don't automatically account for the market impact of actually executing their trades at scale.

These aren't reasons to dismiss the approach — they're the actual research agenda. Comparing agents across market-cap segments and emerging-market conditions (the subject of two manuscripts currently under review) is, in part, an attempt to understand exactly where these limitations bite hardest.

The point of this research isn't to prove machines can out-trade humans. It's to understand, precisely, what a learned policy captures that a fixed rule doesn't — and what it still misses.
