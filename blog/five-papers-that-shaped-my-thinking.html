Five Papers That Shaped How I Think About Reinforcement Learning in Finance

Iqbal K I · January 2026 · 7 min read

People occasionally ask what to read before starting research at the intersection of reinforcement learning and portfolio management. My answer has stabilized into roughly the same five ideas over the last couple of years, so it's worth writing down properly rather than reconstructing it from memory each time.

This isn't a comprehensive literature review — it's a personal shortlist, organized by the specific shift each one caused in how I think about the problem.

1. Markowitz on portfolio selection

Not a reinforcement learning paper at all, but the starting point for understanding what problem everything downstream is actually trying to solve better. You can't evaluate whether a learned policy has found something genuinely new until you understand precisely what the classical efficient-frontier framework already gives you for free — a point I made at more length in a separate post.

2. The foundational deep Q-network work on Atari

Not finance-related on the surface, but the paper that made clear just how far a learned value function could go with raw, high-dimensional input and minimal hand-engineering. The lesson that transferred directly to portfolio management: the state representation matters enormously, and the temptation to hand-engineer features away is usually a temptation to reintroduce the exact assumptions you were trying to avoid by using RL in the first place.

3. Continuous control via policy gradients

Portfolio weights are a continuous action space, and papers built around discrete action spaces don't transfer cleanly. Working through the policy-gradient literature for continuous control was what made algorithms like PPO and DDPG feel like natural tools rather than black boxes borrowed from a different field.

4. Empirical work on regime-switching in asset returns

This is the literature that convinced me volatility regime should be a first-class part of the state representation rather than something a model discovers implicitly, if it's lucky, from enough historical data. It's the direct intellectual predecessor of the regime-aware agent work I eventually published.

5. Critiques of backtesting methodology in quantitative finance

The paper that made me paranoid about overfitting in exactly the productive way. Its central argument — that trying enough strategy variations against the same historical data will eventually produce an impressive-looking backtest by chance alone — reshaped how I design experiments, not just how I evaluate other people's results. It's the reason behind most of the checklist in an earlier post here.

None of these five papers are about reinforcement learning in finance specifically. Between them, they cover almost everything you need to evaluate a paper that is.

If I had to add a sixth category, it would be: read outside your subfield deliberately. The best methodological corrections I've made to my own work came from papers that weren't trying to solve my problem at all.
