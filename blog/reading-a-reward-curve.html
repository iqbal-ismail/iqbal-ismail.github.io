Reading a Reward Curve: What Six Months of Training Logs Taught Me

Iqbal K I · August 2026 · 5 min read

Every deep reinforcement learning paper eventually shows you the same picture: a reward curve climbing, more or less, from bottom-left to top-right. What the picture doesn't show is the several hundred hours spent staring at the noisy, unglamorous version of that curve while it was still being produced — the one that looks less like progress and more like static with a faint upward drift.

I've now spent enough time watching portfolio agents train that I've started to develop opinions about what a reward curve is actually telling me, as distinct from what I want it to be telling me. A few of those opinions, for what they're worth.

The curve lies about convergence

A reward curve that flattens out looks like convergence. Sometimes it is. More often, in my experience, it's the agent settling into a locally stable policy that happens to perform acceptably across the training distribution it's seen so far — which is a different claim entirely. The tell is usually in the action distribution, not the reward number: if allocation weights have also stopped moving, that's a much stronger signal than the reward plateau alone.

Variance is information, not noise

It's tempting to smooth a reward curve heavily before looking at it — moving averages, exponential smoothing, whatever removes the jagged parts. I've started doing the opposite: keeping the raw, high-variance version visible alongside the smoothed one. Sudden spikes in variance during training often line up with the agent encountering a genuinely different market regime in the replay buffer for the first time. That moment is usually more interesting than the eventual average.

Compare curves, not just endpoints

When I'm evaluating a design choice — a different reward shaping term, a different state representation — I've learned not to trust the final reward value alone. Two runs can converge to similar final performance while taking completely different paths, and the path tells you something about robustness that the endpoint doesn't. A run that climbs steadily is, in my experience, more likely to generalize than one that spikes early and holds on.

The reward curve is the agent's résumé, not its transcript. It tells you what happened, not why — and the why is usually where the useful research question is hiding.

None of this replaces proper out-of-sample evaluation, which is where the real test happens. But training diagnostics are cheap, immediate, and — if you're willing to read the messy version instead of the pretty one — genuinely informative about whether an agent is learning something durable or just fitting the run it happens to be on.
