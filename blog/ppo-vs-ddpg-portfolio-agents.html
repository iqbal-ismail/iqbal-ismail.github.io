PPO vs. DDPG for Portfolio Agents: A Practitioner's Field Guide

Iqbal K I · July 2026 · 8 min read

I get some version of this question often enough that it's worth writing down properly: should a portfolio-allocation agent be trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)? Both handle continuous action spaces — which you need for portfolio weights — and both show up regularly in the literature. The honest answer is "it depends on what you're optimizing for," but that's not a useful sentence on its own, so here's the longer version.

DDPG learns faster, and forgets faster too

DDPG's off-policy nature means it can reuse past experience aggressively, which in my runs translates to faster early learning — useful when compute is a constraint. The cost is stability. DDPG agents are notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters, and in a portfolio setting specifically, I've seen them latch onto overconfident, concentrated allocations that happened to work well on a subset of the replay buffer and then fail to unlearn that behaviour cleanly when conditions shift.

PPO is slower and more boring, in a good way

PPO's clipped objective keeps policy updates conservative, which shows up as a less exciting but noticeably more stable training curve. For portfolio allocation, where a single bad update can push weights into an unreasonable region of the simplex, that conservatism has been worth the slower convergence in most of my experiments. It's also easier to reason about during debugging — when something goes wrong, PPO's updates are small enough that you can usually localize which training step introduced the problem.

Where DDPG earns its keep

None of this makes DDPG the wrong choice categorically. When the action space is genuinely continuous and fine-grained control matters — deciding not just "increase equity exposure" but by precisely how much — DDPG's deterministic policy gradient can outperform PPO's stochastic policy once it's tuned properly. The tuning is the catch; it takes meaningfully more experimentation to get DDPG to a reliable state than PPO.

If I had to default to one for a first pass on a new dataset, it's PPO. If I already had a working, well-tuned setup and needed to squeeze out more precision, I'd reach for DDPG.

The volatility-regime work I discussed in an earlier post uses PPO for exactly this reason — the regime-conditioning experiments needed a stable baseline before adding complexity, and PPO gave me one without a separate hyperparameter search for every new state representation I tried.
