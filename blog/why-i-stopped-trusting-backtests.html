Why I Stopped Trusting Backtests (Mostly)

Iqbal K I · June 2026 · 6 min read

Every strategy looks good in the backtest where it was built. That's not cynicism, it's close to a tautology — a backtest is, among other things, a report on how well a strategy fits the data it was designed or tuned against. The interesting question was never "does this backtest look good," it's "how much of that good look survives contact with data the strategy never got to see."

I don't think backtests are useless — they're the closest thing this field has to a lab experiment. But I've become considerably more careful about what I let a backtest convince me of. A short checklist I now run before trusting one, mine included.

How many decisions were made looking at this window?

If a reward function, a feature set, or a hyperparameter was adjusted after looking at performance on the test period — even once, even informally — that period has been leaked into the model, however indirectly. The honest fix is a true holdout that nobody, including me, looks at until the model design is completely frozen. It's an uncomfortable amount of discipline to maintain in practice.

What does the strategy do in the worst 5% of days?

Aggregate metrics — Sharpe ratio, cumulative return — average away exactly the days that matter most for whether a strategy is usable. I now look at the worst-decile drawdown days specifically, and ask whether the strategy's behaviour on those days is something I could defend to a risk committee, not just something that nets out acceptably over the full period.

Would a naive baseline have done almost as well?

An uncomfortable number of DRL portfolio results, when compared honestly against an equal-weight or minimum-variance baseline over the same period, show a smaller edge than the headline metric suggests. That's not a reason to abandon the approach — it's a reason to report the baseline comparison every time, which I've made a standing rule for my own work after being embarrassed by skipping it early on.

A backtest can tell you a strategy isn't obviously broken. It can't tell you the strategy will work — that claim needs live or out-of-sample evidence, and even then, only provisionally.

None of this is a novel insight — quantitative finance has been having this argument with itself for decades. But it's easy to forget under deadline pressure, and I've found that writing the checklist down somewhere I have to read again is more effective than trusting myself to remember it.
