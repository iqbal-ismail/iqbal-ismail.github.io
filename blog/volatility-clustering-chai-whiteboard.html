Volatility Clustering, Explained With Chai and a Whiteboard

Iqbal K I · April 2026 · 5 min read

I ended up explaining volatility clustering to a friend outside finance over chai, using none of the notation I'd normally reach for, and it turned out to be a clearer explanation than the one I give in seminars. Writing it down here in roughly the same words.

The idea, without the equations

Calm days tend to follow calm days. Turbulent days tend to follow turbulent days. That's it — that's volatility clustering. It doesn't say anything about which direction the market moves, only that big moves (up or down) tend to arrive in clusters rather than being scattered randomly through time. A market that had a wild Tuesday is more likely to have a wild Wednesday than a market that had a calm Tuesday.

Why it matters more than it sounds like it should

If volatility were constant, or purely random day to day, a huge amount of risk management infrastructure would look completely different. GARCH-family models exist specifically because volatility clusters — they let today's volatility estimate depend on recent volatility, rather than treating every day as drawn independently from the same distribution. That single structural choice is responsible for a large share of why volatility forecasting works at all.

Where it shows up in my own work

This is also the entire premise behind conditioning a portfolio agent's state on volatility regime rather than treating the market as stationary. If turbulence really does cluster, then an agent that can detect it's currently inside a turbulent cluster has genuinely useful information — not just noise — to act on. The empirical question I care about isn't whether clustering exists; that's well established. It's whether a learned policy can exploit the clustering usefully before the cluster ends, which is a much harder and more interesting problem.

"Calm follows calm, storms follow storms" is the whole idea. Everything past that sentence is just how precisely you want to model it.

My friend's response, for the record, was "so it's like moods." Not a bad first approximation, honestly.
