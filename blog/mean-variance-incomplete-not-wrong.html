Mean-Variance Optimization Isn't Wrong, It's Just Incomplete

Iqbal K I · February 2026 · 6 min read

It's become almost reflexive in machine-learning-adjacent finance circles to open a paper by noting the limitations of Markowitz mean-variance optimization before presenting something more sophisticated. I've done it myself. Having spent a few years now building the "something more sophisticated" side of that sentence, I want to push back gently on how that framing is usually deployed.

The assumptions were always meant to be starting points

Mean-variance optimization assumes returns are well-characterized by their first two moments and that the covariance structure is stable enough to estimate and rely on. Those assumptions are frequently violated in real markets — nobody serious disputes that. But Markowitz's 1952 framework was never presented as a complete description of how markets work; it was a formalization of a trade-off that had previously only been reasoned about informally. Judging it as though it claimed to be a final answer is judging it against a standard it never set for itself.

What it got structurally right

The core insight — that diversification benefit depends on covariance, not just individual asset risk, and that this creates an efficient frontier rather than a single "best" portfolio — remains correct as a structural claim, independent of whether variance is the right risk measure or whether the covariance estimate is stable. Deep reinforcement learning agents that learn to condition allocation on cross-asset relationships are, in a real sense, learning a generalization of the same underlying insight, just without committing to a parametric form for it upfront.

Where the real gap is

The honest critique isn't that Markowitz was wrong about diversification. It's that variance is an incomplete risk measure, covariance estimates are unstable in practice, and the framework has no native way to condition on regime. Those are exactly the gaps that volatility-regime-aware and learned approaches are trying to close — which makes them extensions of the mean-variance insight in a meaningful sense, not replacements for it.

Every model I've built since starts from the same question Markowitz asked: how much am I actually being compensated for the risk I'm taking. The answer has just gotten more complicated to compute.

I don't think this is a controversial position among people who've actually implemented both approaches. It's mostly a plea to stop using "mean-variance is outdated" as a free rhetorical move in an introduction, when the more precise and more interesting claim is about which of its specific assumptions break down, and when.
